Overview & Etymology

Etymology "Cardiovascular" comes from the Greek kardia (καρδία = heart) + Latin vasculum (small vessel, diminutive of vas = vessel). The word "heart" itself derives from Proto-Germanic *hertô, sharing a root with the Greek kardia and Latin cor/cordis — the source of words like "coronary" (corona = crown, referring to vessels that crown the heart) and "accord."

The cardiovascular system (CVS) is a closed, double-circuit, pressure-driven transport system consisting of a muscular pump (the heart), a high-pressure arterial distribution network, a vast capillary exchange network, and a low-pressure venous return system. Its fundamental purpose is the continuous delivery of oxygen (O₂), nutrients, hormones, and immune cells to every tissue, alongside the removal of carbon dioxide (CO₂), metabolic waste, and heat.

In adults, the heart beats approximately 60–100 times per minute, pumping around 5 liters of blood per minute at rest — a volume called the Cardiac Output (CO). Over a lifetime of 70 years, this amounts to roughly 2.5 billion heartbeats and 200 million liters of blood pumped — without a single scheduled rest.

The Two Circuits

The CVS operates as two separate but connected circuits:

Circuit Pump Direction Pressure Purpose
Pulmonary (Lesser) Right ventricle Heart → Lungs → Heart ~25/8 mmHg Gas exchange (CO₂ out, O₂ in)
Systemic (Greater) Left ventricle Heart → Body → Heart ~120/80 mmHg Nutrient/O₂ delivery, waste removal
Key Principle

The pulmonary circulation is a low-pressure, high-flow system. This is critical: high pulmonary pressures would cause fluid to leak into the alveoli (pulmonary edema). The right ventricle generates only ~25% of the pressure of the left ventricle. When the right ventricle must pump against elevated pulmonary pressures (pulmonary arterial hypertension), it progressively fails — called cor pulmonale (cor = heart, pulmonale = of the lung).

Physical Principles: Why the System Works

Blood flow obeys the same physics as any fluid. Three fundamental principles govern cardiovascular function:

1. Ohm's Law for Circulation: Just as electrical current (I) = Voltage (V) / Resistance (R), blood flow (Q) = Pressure difference (ΔP) / Resistance (R). The heart creates a pressure gradient; resistance is mostly determined by vessel caliber.

2. Poiseuille's Law: Flow through a tube is proportional to the fourth power of the radius. This means halving a vessel's radius reduces flow by a factor of 16. This is why even small degrees of coronary artery narrowing (stenosis) can be catastrophically significant.

Poiseuille's Law
Q = (π × r⁴ × ΔP) / (8 × η × L)
Q = volumetric flow rate (mL/s) | r = vessel radius (cm) | ΔP = pressure gradient (mmHg) | η (eta) = blood viscosity | L = vessel length (cm).

The r⁴ term is the most important: a 50% reduction in radius reduces flow to 1/16th (0.5⁴ = 0.0625). This explains why small plaques can cause large symptoms.
Exercise 1.1 — Poiseuille's Law
Clinical Scenario

A coronary artery has a normal radius of 2 mm. An atherosclerotic plaque reduces its radius to 1 mm. By what factor does blood flow decrease, assuming pressure gradient and viscosity remain constant?

Show Solution
Flow ∝ r⁴
Normal flow: Q₁ ∝ (2)⁴ = 16
Diseased flow: Q₂ ∝ (1)⁴ = 1
Ratio: Flow is reduced to 1/16th (6.25%) of normal.

Clinical implication: A 50% reduction in radius = 94% reduction in flow. This is why coronary artery disease (CAD) symptoms often appear suddenly — small additional narrowing produces dramatic drops in flow. A 75% stenosis of a coronary artery (reducing radius by 50%) will reduce resting flow by 94%, causing angina even at rest.

3. Laplace's Law: Wall tension (T) = Pressure (P) × Radius (r) / Wall thickness (h). A dilated heart (larger radius) must generate more wall tension to create the same pressure, consuming more oxygen. This is why a dilated, failing heart is so energetically inefficient — a vicious cycle.

Laplace's Law (Cardiac Application)
T = (P × r) / (2h)
T = myocardial wall tension (afterload felt by each muscle fiber) | P = intraventricular pressure | r = ventricular radius | h = wall thickness.

This explains two compensatory mechanisms in heart failure: (1) Hypertrophy (increasing h) reduces wall tension per fiber. (2) Vasoconstriction and diuresis (reducing r and P) reduce wall tension. It also explains why ACE inhibitors (which reduce P) and beta-blockers (which reduce r over time) are beneficial.

Embryology of the Heart

Etymology Embryology: from Greek embryon (ἔμβρυον = that which grows within) + -logia (study of). The prefix em- means "within" and bryo- comes from bryein (to swell, to be full). The heart is the first functional organ in the human embryo.

The heart begins forming at approximately day 15–16 of gestation from splanchnic mesoderm — the middle embryonic layer surrounding the gut. By day 22, it is already beating. This extraordinary precocity reflects the embryo's urgent metabolic need: as soon as diffusion alone cannot supply oxygen to all cells, a pumping system must exist.

Stages of Cardiac Development

Days 15–18
Cardiac Crescent

Bilateral cardiogenic fields in the splanchnic mesoderm fuse to form a horseshoe-shaped cardiac crescent. These precursor cells express transcription factors Nkx2.5 and GATA4 — the "master switches" of cardiac differentiation. Mutations in Nkx2.5 cause congenital heart defects (CHDs) including atrial septal defects (ASDs) and conduction system abnormalities. (Bruneau, 2002)

Days 20–22
Linear Heart Tube

The crescent folds and fuses in the midline to form a straight endocardial tube. From cranial to caudal: truncus arteriosus → bulbus cordis → primitive ventricle → primitive atrium → sinus venosus. This tube begins to beat spontaneously at day 21–22, driven by pacemaker cells even before innervation exists. The mechanism: If (funny current) — voltage-gated sodium/potassium channels (HCN4 channels) that generate spontaneous depolarization.

Days 23–28
Cardiac Looping

The linear tube undergoes rightward D-looping — bending to the right (dextral). This is one of the first breaks in bilateral symmetry in the embryo, driven by leftward nodal flow at the embryonic node (cilia beating leftward, driven by motor protein Dynein). The atria shift posteriorly and superiorly; the ventricles move anteriorly and inferiorly. Situs inversus (mirror-image organs) occurs when looping goes to the left (L-looping), as in Kartagener syndrome (Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia, PCD) where dynein arms are defective.

Weeks 4–8
Septation & Valve Formation

Four septa form simultaneously: (1) the atrial septum (septum primum, then secundum, creating the foramen ovale); (2) the ventricular septum (muscular + membranous portions); (3) the atrioventricular (AV) septum from endocardial cushions; (4) the aorticopulmonary septum spiraling to divide the truncus arteriosus into aorta and pulmonary trunk. Endocardial cushion cells undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to form valve leaflets — a process requiring TGF-β signaling.

Week 8 onward
Fetal Circulation Adaptations

Three fetal shunts exist: foramen ovale (right → left atrium, bypassing pulmonary circulation); ductus arteriosus (pulmonary trunk → aorta, bypassing lungs); ductus venosus (umbilical vein → IVC, bypassing liver). All normally close at birth when pulmonary vascular resistance falls and left-sided pressures exceed right-sided. Failure of closure = patent foramen ovale (PFO), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), persistent fetal circulation.

Clinical Application — Congenital Heart Defects
Frequency and Classification

Congenital heart defects (CHDs) affect approximately 8–10 per 1,000 live births, making them the most common congenital malformations. They are classified as:

  • Acyanotic (left-to-right shunts initially): VSD (ventricular septal defect), ASD, PDA, atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD)
  • Cyanotic (right-to-left shunts): Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), truncus arteriosus
  • Obstructive lesions: Pulmonary stenosis, aortic stenosis, coarctation of the aorta

In Eisenmenger syndrome, a long-standing left-to-right shunt causes pulmonary hypertension severe enough to reverse flow to right-to-left, causing late-onset cyanosis. This is irreversible without transplantation — which is why early repair of shunts is critical.

Gross Anatomy of the Heart

Etymology "Gross anatomy": from Latin grossus (thick, coarse) — referring to structures visible to the naked eye, as opposed to microscopic anatomy. The anatomical term apex comes from Latin meaning "tip" or "peak"; base from Greek basis (foundation, that which one stands on). Paradoxically, the cardiac apex points downward-left, and the base faces upward-right.

Position and Orientation

The heart is located in the middle mediastinum (the central compartment of the chest between the two pleural cavities), slightly left of the midline. It is roughly the size of a clenched fist and weighs 250–350 g in adults (heavier in men, athletes, and those with hypertension). Its dimensions: approximately 12 cm long, 8–9 cm wide, 6 cm in anteroposterior depth.

The heart's position is best described by its surfaces:

Clinical Pearl — Cardiac Surface Anatomy

Because the right ventricle forms most of the anterior surface, blunt chest trauma (steering wheel injuries) most commonly contuses the RV, not the LV. Conversely, posterior MI (posterior STEMI) involves the posterior wall (mainly LV) and is reflected on the LA/esophageal surface — it may cause dysphagia or be visible on ECG as posterior changes (tall R in V1, ST depression V1-V3, which are "reciprocal" changes).

The Apex and Base

The apex of the heart points to the left, downward, and forward. It is formed entirely by the left ventricle and sits at the level of the 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line. This is the site of the apex beat (point of maximal impulse, PMI) — the palpable cardiac impulse produced by the LV hitting the chest wall during systole. A displaced PMI (lateral or inferior) suggests cardiomegaly.

The base is the posterior superior surface, formed mainly by the left atrium. The four pulmonary veins enter the LA at the base. It is NOT the "bottom" of the heart — a common misconception from the Latin basis as foundation, but in cardiology the base is actually the superior-posterior aspect.

Sulci (Grooves)

The heart's external surface shows grooves (sulci) marking the boundaries between chambers:

  • Coronary sulcus (atrioventricular groove): Encircles the heart, separating atria from ventricles. Contains the right coronary artery (RCA), the coronary sinus, and circumflex branch of the left coronary artery (LCA).
  • Anterior interventricular sulcus: On the sternocostal surface, contains the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and great cardiac vein.
  • Posterior interventricular sulcus: On the diaphragmatic surface, contains the posterior descending artery (PDA) and middle cardiac vein.

Heart Chambers — Anatomy & Function

Right Atrium (RA)

The RA receives deoxygenated systemic venous blood from the superior vena cava (SVC) (draining the upper body), inferior vena cava (IVC) (draining the lower body), and the coronary sinus (draining the heart's own venous return). Its thin wall (2–3 mm) reflects its low-pressure function.

Internal features of the RA include: the crista terminalis — a muscular ridge separating the smooth posterior sinus venarum from the anterior trabeculated atrium (derived from the embryonic right atrium); pectinate muscles (from Latin pecten = comb, due to their comb-like appearance); and the fossa ovalis, the oval depression marking the site of the fetal foramen ovale (a patent foramen ovale, PFO — present in ~25% of adults — may paradoxically transmit thrombi from veins to the systemic circulation, causing cryptogenic stroke).

Right Ventricle (RV)

The RV has a crescent-shaped cross-section (wrapping around the LV), with a wall thickness of 3–5 mm. It pumps against low pulmonary pressures (~25 mmHg systolic). Its trabeculae carneae (Latin: trabecula = little beam, carnea = fleshy) give its inner surface a rough appearance. The moderator band (septomarginal trabecula) carries part of the right bundle branch to the anterior papillary muscle — clinically important as it may house arrhythmogenic foci.

The RV has three parts: inlet (receiving blood from RA), trabecular body, and outlet (infundibulum/conus arteriosus leading to the pulmonary valve). This is different from the LV which has inlet and outlet components but no distinct infundibulum.

Left Atrium (LA)

The LA forms the majority of the heart's posterior surface (base). It receives oxygenated blood from four pulmonary veins (two from each lung). Wall thickness ~3 mm. It is the most posterior structure of the heart — which is why it can be compressed by an enlarged heart or masses, and why a dilated LA (e.g., in mitral stenosis) can compress the esophagus (causing dysphagia) or the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (causing hoarseness). The LA appendage (LAA) is a blind pouch with high risk for thrombus formation in atrial fibrillation (AF).

Left Ventricle (LV)

The LV is the main systemic pump, with walls 8–12 mm thick (roughly 3× the RV), reflecting the much higher systemic pressures it generates (~120 mmHg systolic). Its ellipsoidal shape and thick walls make it highly efficient.

Key structures:

  • Papillary muscles: anterolateral and posteromedial — attached via chordae tendineae to the mitral valve leaflets, preventing leaflet prolapse into the LA during systole. The posteromedial papillary muscle has a single blood supply (usually from RCA), making it more vulnerable to ischemic rupture in inferior MI — producing sudden severe mitral regurgitation (MR).
  • Interventricular septum (IVS): 10–12 mm thick; composed of a muscular portion and a membranous portion. The membranous septum is the last part to close in embryogenesis and the most common site of VSDs.
  • Outflow tract (LVOT): between the anterior mitral leaflet and the IVS; site of subaortic stenosis in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM).

Etymology Valve: from Latin valva = leaf of a folding door, from volvere = to roll/turn. They are one-way gates ensuring unidirectional blood flow. The heart has four valves, grouped as two AV valves (atrioventricular — between atria and ventricles) and two semilunar valves (between ventricles and great arteries, named for their half-moon-shaped cusps).

Microscopic Structure of Valve Leaflets

Histology — Valve Leaflet Layers

Cardiac valve leaflets have a trilaminar structure (three layers) — from ventricular surface to atrial/arterial surface:

  1. Fibrosa: The load-bearing layer facing high-pressure outflow. Dense irregular collagen type I arranged circumferentially. Provides the tensile strength to resist regurgitant forces. Located on the aortic side of the aortic valve, ventricular side of mitral valve.
  2. Spongiosa: Middle loose connective tissue layer containing proteoglycans (glycosaminoglycans) and hydrated ground substance — acts as a shock absorber, distributing mechanical stress. In myxomatous degeneration (cause of mitral valve prolapse), this layer expands excessively.
  3. Ventricularis/Atrialis: Thin layer facing the blood flow. Contains elastin fibers allowing the leaflet to flex with each heartbeat. Covered by a single layer of valve interstitial cells (VICs) resembling smooth muscle cells — capable of remodeling the matrix in disease.

The entire leaflet is covered by valve endothelial cells (VECs), which differ from vascular endothelium: they are aligned perpendicular to blood flow (vs. parallel in arteries) and have distinct gene expression patterns. VEC dysfunction triggers calcification in aortic stenosis.

The Aortic Valve — Clinical Focus

The aortic valve has three semilunar cusps: left (L), right (R), and non-coronary (N), named for their relationship to the coronary ostia. The left and right coronary arteries arise from the left and right aortic sinuses of Valsalva respectively. The non-coronary cusp faces the right atrium and left atrium.

A bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) — present in 1–2% of the population, the most common congenital heart defect — has only two cusps and is associated with progressive aortic stenosis (AS), aortic regurgitation (AR), and aortic root dilation/dissection. BAV is associated with mutations in the NOTCH1 gene.

Clinical Application — Murmur Localization by Valve Area

Each valve has a classical auscultation area where its murmur is best heard (sound travels in the direction of blood flow):

  • Aortic area: 2nd right intercostal space (RICS), right sternal border
  • Pulmonary area: 2nd left intercostal space (LICS), left sternal border
  • Tricuspid area: 4th–5th LICS, left sternal border
  • Mitral area (apex): 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line (MCL)

Aortic stenosis (AS) produces a harsh, crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the aortic area, radiating to the carotids. Mitral regurgitation (MR) produces a holosystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. Erb's point (3rd LICS) is used to auscultate both aortic regurgitation (AR, heard as a decrescendo diastolic murmur leaning forward, breath held in expiration) and sounds from all four valves.

Coronary Circulation

Etymology Coronary: from Latin corona (crown) — because the coronary arteries encircle the heart like a crown when viewed from above. The term dates to the Renaissance anatomists who described them as "coronaria arteria." Angina: from Latin meaning "choking, strangling pain" (from angere = to choke). Infarction: from Latin infarctus = stuffed, from infarcire = to stuff in — referring to dead tissue "stuffed" with blood that cannot circulate.

The heart cannot rely on diffusion alone for its own oxygen supply — no other organ has such high and continuous metabolic demands. The coronary arteries deliver approximately 250 mL of blood per minute at rest, rising to 1,000–1,200 mL/min during maximal exercise (a 4-5× increase called coronary flow reserve, CFR). The myocardium extracts 70–75% of delivered oxygen at rest — compared to ~25% by skeletal muscle. This means the heart cannot significantly increase oxygen extraction as a response to increased demand; it must increase blood flow. When flow cannot increase (stenosis), ischemia ensues.

Coronary Arterial Anatomy

Two main coronary arteries arise from the aortic sinuses:

Left Coronary Artery (LCA) — arises from the left aortic sinus, has a short main stem (left main coronary artery, LMCA, 5–15 mm long) before dividing into:

  • Left Anterior Descending (LAD): Runs in the anterior interventricular sulcus toward the apex. Supplies the anterior LV wall, anterior 2/3 of the interventricular septum, and anterior papillary muscle. Via septal perforators and diagonal branches. Called the "widow maker" because its occlusion causes large anterior MI with high mortality.
  • Left Circumflex (LCx): Runs in the left coronary sulcus. Supplies the lateral and posterior LV wall via obtuse marginal (OM) branches. In ~15% of people (left-dominant circulation), the LCx gives rise to the posterior descending artery (PDA).

Right Coronary Artery (RCA) — arises from the right aortic sinus, runs in the right coronary sulcus. Supplies the RA, most of the RV, the inferior LV wall, and posterior 1/3 of the IVS. In ~85% of people (right-dominant circulation), the RCA gives rise to the PDA and the posterior left ventricular branches (PLV). The RCA also supplies the SA node (60% of people) and AV node (85-90% of people) — explaining why inferior MI (RCA territory) can cause sinus bradycardia, AV block, and right bundle branch block (RBBB).

Clinical Key — Coronary Dominance

Right-dominant: ~85% — RCA supplies the PDA and posterior LV.
Left-dominant: ~8% — LCx supplies the PDA and posterior LV.
Co-dominant: ~7% — both RCA and LCx contribute to PDA.

Dominance determines which vessel to open urgently in inferoposterior MI. In right-dominant patients (most people), inferior ST elevation (II, III, aVF) = RCA occlusion. In left-dominant patients, the same ECG pattern might indicate LCx occlusion (which also carries worse prognosis since LCx in left-dominant patients is a large vessel).

Coronary Venous System

Coronary veins drain into the coronary sinus, which empties into the RA between the IVC and the tricuspid valve. Three main veins: the great cardiac vein (parallel to the LAD), the middle cardiac vein (parallel to the PDA), and the small cardiac vein (parallel to the RCA marginal branch). About 20% of venous drainage occurs via Thebesian veins — tiny vessels draining directly into the cardiac chambers, predominantly the right heart. These provide a small right-to-left shunt and contribute to the normal AV oxygen difference.

Coronary Physiology — Autoregulation

Coronary blood flow is autoregulated — maintained relatively constant across a perfusion pressure range of ~60–140 mmHg. Below 60 mmHg, autoregulation fails and ischemia occurs. The main regulators of coronary tone:

  • Metabolic (most important): Adenosine (from ATP breakdown), CO₂, H⁺, K⁺, and hypoxia all cause vasodilation. This matches blood flow to metabolic demand automatically.
  • Myogenic: Increased wall tension causes smooth muscle contraction (Bayliss effect).
  • Endothelial: Shear stress → nitric oxide (NO) → vasodilation. Acetylcholine → NO → vasodilation (opposite to its effect on non-endothelialized vessels). In atherosclerosis, dysfunctional endothelium produces less NO → vasospasm.
  • Neural: Sympathetic β₂ activation → vasodilation (dominant over α₁ vasoconstriction).
Coronary Perfusion Pressure
CPP = Aortic DBP − LVEDP
CPP = Coronary Perfusion Pressure | DBP = Diastolic Blood Pressure | LVEDP = Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Pressure

The coronary arteries fill primarily during diastole, because during systole the myocardium contracts and compresses the intramural vessels, especially in the subendocardium. This is unique to the heart. A normal CPP is ~60–80 mmHg.

Clinical implication: In aortic regurgitation, the DBP is very low (wide pulse pressure) → low CPP → subendocardial ischemia despite normal or high systolic BP. In tachycardia, diastole is shortened → less time for coronary filling → ischemia. Both explain why beta-blockers (slow HR, increase diastole duration, and may slightly increase DBP) are beneficial in stable angina.

Cardiac Muscle — Histology

Etymology Myocardium: from Greek myo- (μῦς = muscle) + kardia (heart) — the muscular layer of the heart. Cardiomyocyte: from kardia + myo- + Greek kytos (κύτος = container, cell). Sarcomere: from Greek sarkos (σάρκος = flesh) + meros (μέρος = part) — the basic contractile unit of striated muscle. Intercalated disc: from Latin intercalare = to insert between.

The Cardiomyocyte

Cardiomyocytes are the working contractile cells of the heart. They have unique properties that distinguish them from skeletal and smooth muscle:

Histology — Cardiomyocyte Features
  • Size: 10–20 μm diameter, 50–100 μm long (larger in hypertrophy: up to 40 μm diameter)
  • Nuclei: 1–2 central nuclei (unlike skeletal muscle: multiple peripheral nuclei; unlike smooth muscle: single central nucleus). In heart failure, polyploidy increases — cells have >2 copies of DNA
  • Branching: Cardiomyocytes branch and interconnect, forming a three-dimensional syncytium
  • Striation: Cross-striations due to regular sarcomere organization (same as skeletal muscle)
  • Mitochondria: Extremely abundant — 30–40% of cell volume (vs. ~2% in skeletal muscle). This reflects the heart's enormous energy demands and purely aerobic metabolism
  • T-tubules: Present (unlike atrial cells of some species) but less extensive than skeletal muscle. Wide T-tubule openings allow efficient Ca²⁺ signaling
  • Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR): Less developed than skeletal muscle — making cardiac muscle more dependent on extracellular Ca²⁺ entry
  • Lipid droplets: Present — stored free fatty acids (FFAs), the primary fuel (~60–70% of ATP production from FFA oxidation)

The Sarcomere — In Detail

The sarcomere is the fundamental contractile unit, bounded by two Z-discs (from German Zwischenscheibe = intermediate disc). It contains:

  • Thick filaments (myosin): 1.6 μm long, 15 nm diameter. Each myosin molecule consists of two heavy chains forming a coiled-coil tail, and two pairs of light chains at the head. The globular myosin head (S1 subfragment) is the molecular motor — it binds actin and hydrolyzes ATP to generate force. Cardiac myosin heavy chain (MHC) exists as two isoforms: β-MHC (slow, dominant in adult human ventricle, energy-efficient) and α-MHC (fast, dominant in atria). In heart failure, there is a shift from α to β-MHC — paradoxically an adaptive response to reduce energy expenditure.
  • Thin filaments (actin): 1.0 μm long, 7 nm diameter. F-actin (filamentous actin) is a double-stranded helix of G-actin monomers. The troponin-tropomyosin complex regulates actin-myosin interaction: tropomyosin lies in the groove of the actin helix, physically blocking myosin binding sites at rest. Troponin I (inhibitory), T (tropomyosin-binding), and C (calcium-binding) form the regulatory switch.
  • Titin: The largest protein in the human body (3.7 MDa), spanning from Z-disc to M-line. Acts as a molecular spring determining sarcomere passive stiffness. Titin mutations are the most common cause of familial dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). Phosphorylation by protein kinase A (PKA) reduces titin stiffness (contributing to the Frank-Starling mechanism).
  • M-line proteins: Myomesin, M-protein — anchor thick filaments at the center.
  • Zones: A-band (entire thick filament + overlapping thin filaments), I-band (thin filaments only, shortens during contraction), H-zone (thick filaments only, shortens during contraction), M-line (center of A-band, does not change).

Intercalated Discs

The intercalated disc (ICD) is the specialized junction between cardiomyocytes — a dark transverse line visible on hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. It contains three junction types:

  • Gap junctions (connexons): Ion channels made of connexin 43 (CX43, encoded by GJA1 gene) that allow direct cell-to-cell passage of ions and small molecules (MW <1000 Da). This creates electrical coupling — making the myocardium a functional syncytium. An action potential in one cell spreads to all connected cells. Mutations in CX43 cause arrhythmias.
  • Fascia adherens: The mechanical anchoring junction, linking actin filaments of adjacent cells via N-cadherin and catenins. Transmits contractile force between cells.
  • Desmosomes: Link intermediate filaments (desmin) — the "spot welds" resisting lateral mechanical stress. Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), previously called ARVC (arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy), is caused by mutations in desmosomal proteins (plakophilin-2, desmoplakin, desmoglein, etc.), leading to cardiomyocyte detachment, fibro-fatty replacement, and life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.
Clinical Application — Cardiac Biomarkers
Troponin: Why It's the Gold Standard

Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and troponin T (cTnT) are cardiac-specific isoforms — not expressed in healthy skeletal muscle. When cardiomyocytes die (necrosis), the troponin complex is released into the bloodstream. This makes high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) the most sensitive and specific biomarker for myocardial infarction:

  • Rises within 1–3 hours of MI onset
  • Peaks at 12–24 hours
  • Returns to normal in 7–14 days
  • The "0h/3h" algorithm using hs-cTnT can rule in or rule out NSTEMI (Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction) with >99% sensitivity

The 2019 ESC (European Society of Cardiology) NSTEMI guidelines recommend the 0/1h or 0/2h rapid rule-out algorithm with hs-cTn. (Collet et al., EHJ 2021)

Electrophysiology — The Conduction System

Etymology Electrophysiology: from Greek elektron (ἤλεκτρον = amber, source of static electricity) + physis (φύσις = nature) + logia (study). The conduction system is named for its discoverers: Keith and Flack (SA node, 1907), Sunao Tawara (AV node and bundle, 1906), Wilhelm His Jr. (bundle of His, 1893), Johannes Purkinje (Purkinje fibers, 1845).

The Conduction System

The cardiac conduction system is a specialized network of modified cardiomyocytes that generate and coordinate electrical impulses, ensuring the orderly sequence of atrial then ventricular contraction. It consists of:

The SA Node — Molecular Pacemaking

The SA node fires spontaneously because its cells lack a stable resting membrane potential. Instead, they undergo a spontaneous depolarization (pacemaker potential) during diastole. This is called automaticity — the ability to self-generate action potentials without external stimulation.

The molecular basis of automaticity involves three key currents:

  1. If ("funny current", HCN channels): A mixed Na⁺/K⁺ inward current activated at negative potentials (−60 to −40 mV). "Funny" because it is activated by hyperpolarization, opposite to most channels. HCN4 channels (hyperpolarization-activated, cyclic nucleotide–gated channels) are responsible. cAMP (from β₁ adrenergic stimulation) shifts the activation threshold rightward → faster depolarization → faster heart rate. Ivabradine (a drug used in HF and stable angina) specifically blocks If → slows HR without reducing contractility.
  2. ICaL (L-type calcium current): Responsible for the upstroke of the SA node action potential (NOT fast sodium channels, unlike working myocardium). This is why calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) dramatically slow SA node firing and AV node conduction.
  3. IKr/IKs (delayed rectifier K⁺ currents): Repolarize the cell. If blocked (e.g., by some antiarrhythmics), action potential prolongs and the QT interval lengthens — risk of Torsades de Pointes.

AV Node — The Gateway

The AV node introduces a physiologically critical delay of ~0.1–0.12 seconds. This delay ensures atria fully empty before ventricles contract. Without this delay, the ventricles would begin contracting before atrial filling is complete, reducing cardiac output by ~20–30%.

The AV node is the only normal electrical connection between atria and ventricles (the fibrous cardiac skeleton electrically insulates the two). This means:

  • In atrial fibrillation (AF), the ventricular rate is determined by how many impulses the AV node conducts through — a natural rate controller
  • Ablation of the AV node can control ventricular rate in refractory AF (requires permanent pacemaker)
  • Accessory pathways (e.g., Bundle of Kent in Wolff-Parkinson-White, WPW syndrome) bypass the AV node → pre-excitation, delta wave, short PR on ECG, and risk of very fast conduction during AF → ventricular fibrillation (VF)
Clinical Application — Heart Blocks

AV blocks are classified by degree:

  • 1st degree: Prolonged PR >200 ms (0.20 s). All P waves conducted. Usually benign (vagal tone, inferior MI, drugs)
  • 2nd degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach): Progressive PR prolongation until a P wave is not conducted (QRS dropped). Block usually at the AV node. Often benign, can be caused by inferior MI (RCA supplies AV node), usually transient.
  • 2nd degree Mobitz II: Fixed PR interval with sudden non-conducted P waves. Block usually below the AV node (infra-Hisian). More dangerous — can progress to complete heart block. Usually requires permanent pacemaker.
  • 3rd degree (complete heart block, CHB): Complete dissociation between P waves and QRS. Atria and ventricles beat independently. A junctional or ventricular escape rhythm maintains the heartbeat. Requires immediate pacemaker (temporary or permanent).

Cardiac Action Potential

The cardiac action potential (AP) is a rapid, stereotyped change in membrane voltage that triggers contraction. Unlike the SA/AV nodal AP (driven by Ca²⁺), the ventricular cardiomyocyte AP is driven primarily by Na⁺ and has five distinct phases:

Phase 4 — Resting Membrane Potential
−90 mV (stable in ventricle; not stable in SA node)

Maintained by IK1 (inward rectifier K⁺ current — dominant K⁺ conductance at rest). The cell interior is negative because K⁺ diffuses out down its concentration gradient, leaving behind negative charges. SA node cells lack IK1 — hence no stable resting potential, hence automaticity.

Phase 0 — Rapid Depolarization
−90 mV → +30 mV (upstroke)

Massive opening of voltage-gated fast Na⁺ channels (Nav1.5) when threshold (~−65 mV) is reached. Na⁺ rushes in (Na⁺ concentration outside ~140 mEq/L vs. inside ~10–15 mEq/L). Conduction velocity: 0.5 m/s in AV node; 1–4 m/s in working myocardium; 2–4 m/s in Purkinje fibers. Fast Na⁺ channels are blocked by class I antiarrhythmics (lidocaine, flecainide, quinidine) — used to treat ventricular and atrial arrhythmias.

Phase 1 — Rapid Repolarization
+30 mV → +10 mV (notch)

Inactivation of Na⁺ channels + transient outward K⁺ current (Ito, potassium-to-outward). This creates the characteristic "spike and dome" morphology. Ito is prominent in epicardium (outer) but sparse in endocardium (inner) — creating the transmural voltage gradient underlying the T wave on ECG.

Phase 2 — Plateau
~+10 mV (flat, 0.2–0.3 s)

The cardiac AP's unique feature — no equivalent in nerve or skeletal muscle. Maintained by a balance between: inward L-type Ca²⁺ current (ICaL) driving Ca²⁺ into the cell and triggering contraction AND outward slow K⁺ current (IKr, IKs). The plateau prolongs the refractory period, preventing re-excitation (and therefore preventing tetanic contraction — unlike skeletal muscle, the heart CANNOT go into sustained tetanus, which would be fatal). The QT interval on ECG represents ventricular AP duration. QT prolongation (long QT syndrome, LQTS) extends the vulnerable period for arrhythmia induction.

Phase 3 — Rapid Repolarization
+10 mV → −90 mV

ICaL inactivates; IKr and IKs (delayed rectifier K⁺ currents) dominate → repolarization. IKr is the target of multiple drugs causing QT prolongation (antiarrhythmics: sotalol, amiodarone; antibiotics: azithromycin, fluoroquinolones; antipsychotics: haloperidol; antiemetics: domperidone). Mutations in HERG gene (encoding IKr) cause congenital LQTS type 2.

Nernst Equation — Ion Equilibrium Potential
E_ion = (RT/zF) × ln([ion]outside / [ion]inside)
E_ion = equilibrium potential | R = universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K) | T = absolute temperature (K) | z = ion valence | F = Faraday's constant (96,485 C/mol) | ln = natural logarithm

At 37°C (310 K): RT/F ≈ 26.7 mV. For a monovalent ion (z=1): E = 61.5 × log([out]/[in])

E_K = 61.5 × log(4/140) ≈ −94 mV (explains resting potential — dominated by K⁺)
E_Na = 61.5 × log(140/10) ≈ +70 mV (explains direction of Na⁺ influx during depolarization)
E_Ca = (61.5/2) × log(2/0.0001) ≈ +132 mV (Ca²⁺ strongly driven inward)

The ECG — Electrocardiography

Etymology Electrocardiogram: from Greek elektron + kardia (heart) + gramma (something written). The ECG was invented by Willem Einthoven (1872–1927), a Dutch physiologist who won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1924. He used a string galvanometer and defined the standard limb leads (I, II, III) — still named "Einthoven's leads."

The ECG records the summed electrical activity of all cardiac cells from the body surface. It shows voltage (mV) vs. time (ms). Standard paper speed = 25 mm/s (1 small square = 40 ms = 0.04 s; 1 large square = 200 ms = 0.20 s). Standard calibration: 1 mV = 10 mm.

The 12-Lead ECG System

Standard ECG uses 12 leads viewing the heart from different angles:

  • Limb leads (frontal plane): I, II, III (Einthoven's triangle) + aVR, aVL, aVF (augmented unipolar). Together cover 360° of the frontal plane.
  • Precordial leads (horizontal plane): V1 (right sternal border) to V6 (left mid-axillary line). V1–V2: right ventricle/septum. V3–V4: anterior wall. V5–V6: lateral wall.
  • Additional leads: V7–V9 (posterior MI); V3R–V4R (right-sided, for RV infarction).
STEMI Localization on ECG

ST elevation in specific lead groups identifies the culprit artery:

  • Anterior (V1–V4): LAD occlusion — proximal LAD = "widow maker" (also may have II, III, aVF ST depression)
  • Inferior (II, III, aVF): RCA (85%) or LCx (15%) occlusion
  • Lateral (I, aVL, V5–V6): LCx or diagonal branch
  • Posterior (ST depression V1–V3 + tall R in V1 + positive in V7–V9): LCx or PDA
  • Right ventricular (ST↑ V3R/V4R + inferior ST↑): Proximal RCA occlusion — critical: do NOT give nitrates (can cause severe hypotension in RV infarction since RV is preload-dependent)

The Cardiac Cycle — Pressure, Volume & Time

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of mechanical events (pressure and volume changes) in one heartbeat. At 75 bpm, one cycle takes 0.8 seconds: systole (contraction/ejection) ~0.3 s, diastole (relaxation/filling) ~0.5 s. As HR increases, diastole shortens disproportionately — at 150 bpm, diastole may be only 0.1–0.15 s, critically limiting coronary filling and ventricular filling.

Phases of the Cardiac Cycle (Left Ventricle)

Phase 1 — Atrial Systole
The "atrial kick" — contributes 15–30% of LV filling

Atrial contraction (driven by P wave) boosts LV filling. In atrial fibrillation (AF), the atrial kick is lost — cardiac output decreases by 15–30%. This is especially significant in patients with diastolic dysfunction or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, where the ventricle is stiff and depends more on atrial filling. This explains why AF can precipitate acute pulmonary edema in these patients.

Phase 2 — Isovolumetric Contraction (IVC)
~0.05 s | Volume constant, pressure rises rapidly

After QRS (ventricular depolarization), myocytes contract. LV pressure rises sharply. Both mitral valve (closed — LV pressure > LA pressure) and aortic valve (closed — LV pressure still < aortic diastolic ~80 mmHg) are shut. Volume does NOT change yet — hence "isovolumetric" (from Greek isos = equal). This is the highest rate of energy consumption per unit time. S1 heart sound occurs at mitral (and tricuspid) valve closure.

Phase 3 — Rapid Ejection
~0.15 s | LV pressure peaks ~120 mmHg; SV ejected

When LV pressure exceeds aortic diastolic pressure, the aortic valve opens. ~75% of stroke volume (SV) is ejected in the first third of systole (rapid ejection phase). LV volume decreases from EDV (~120–130 mL) to ESV (~50–60 mL). Ejection fraction (EF) = SV/EDV. Normal EF: 55–70%. EF <40% = reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF — Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction).

Phase 4 — Reduced Ejection
Remaining ~25% of SV ejected; LV pressure begins to fall

Myocyte repolarization begins (T wave). LV pressure falls below aortic pressure — aortic valve snaps closed (S2 heart sound — "lub DUB": S1 at mitral closure, S2 at aortic/pulmonary closure). The dicrotic notch on the aortic pressure trace marks aortic valve closure.

Phase 5 — Isovolumetric Relaxation (IVR)
~0.08 s | Volume constant, pressure falls rapidly

All valves closed. LV pressure falls rapidly as myocytes relax (active process requiring ATP — calcium reuptake by the SR-Ca²⁺-ATPase, SERCA2a, pump). Impaired active relaxation = diastolic dysfunction. IVR is the index of diastolic function: measured by tissue Doppler as e' velocity. In HFpEF (Heart Failure with preserved EF), IVR is prolonged.

Phase 6 — Rapid Filling
~0.15 s | 75–80% of LV filling

When LV pressure falls below LA pressure, the mitral valve opens. Blood rushes into the LV driven by the pressure gradient. S3 (third heart sound) — a low-frequency filling sound — may occur here. S3 in a young healthy person = normal rapid filling (physiologic). S3 in an adult >40 years = pathologic — indicates increased LV filling pressures (HF, MR, VSD). It is the "Ken-tuc-ky" gallop (lub-dub-S3).

Phase 7 — Diastasis
~0.2 s | Slow filling phase

Pressures equalize between LA and LV; flow slows. This phase is most shortened in tachycardia. S4 (fourth heart sound, "Ten-nes-see" gallop) occurs at the end of diastasis during atrial kick in a stiff ventricle — always pathologic (hypertension, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, ischemia). S4 indicates reduced LV compliance.

Clinical Application — The Pressure-Volume Loop
Frank-Starling and PV Loop Analysis

The left ventricular pressure-volume (PV) loop is a graphical representation of one cardiac cycle, plotting LV pressure (mmHg, y-axis) against LV volume (mL, x-axis). The loop moves counterclockwise:

  • Bottom-right corner: End-diastolic point (EDV, ~130 mL, LVEDP ~8 mmHg) — after mitral valve closes
  • Top-right → Top-left: Isovolumetric contraction (vertical rise), then ejection (horizontal left movement)
  • Top-left corner: End-systolic point (ESV, ~50 mL, LV pressure ~120 mmHg) — at aortic valve closure
  • Bottom-left → Bottom-right: Isovolumetric relaxation (vertical fall), then filling (horizontal right movement)

The end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) — the slope connecting ESV at different loads — represents contractility (preload/afterload-independent). Shifts left/up = positive inotropy (dobutamine, digoxin). Shifts right/down = negative inotropy (heart failure, beta-blockers in acute HF). The area enclosed by the PV loop = stroke work (in mL·mmHg, convertible to Joules).

Cardiac Output, Stroke Volume & Determinants

Cardiac Output Formula
CO = HR × SV
CO = Cardiac Output (L/min) | HR = Heart Rate (beats/min) | SV = Stroke Volume (mL/beat)

Normal CO at rest: ~5 L/min (range 4–8 L/min)
Normal SV: ~70 mL (range 60–100 mL)
Normal HR: 60–100 bpm

Cardiac Index (CI) = CO / Body Surface Area (BSA) — corrects for body size. Normal CI: 2.5–4.0 L/min/m². CI <2.2 L/min/m² = cardiogenic shock.

During maximal exercise, CO can reach 20–25 L/min in trained athletes (up to 40 L/min in elite cyclists) — driven by both increased HR (up to 200 bpm) and increased SV (from ~70 mL to ~120–130 mL via Starling mechanism and reduced afterload from peripheral vasodilation).

Three Determinants of Stroke Volume

1. Preload — the degree of ventricular filling before contraction (end-diastolic volume, EDV). Clinically approximated by central venous pressure (CVP) for RV, or LVEDP/PCWP for LV. Increased by: fluid loading, increased venous return (exercise), decreased HR (more time to fill), aortic regurgitation. Decreased by: dehydration, hemorrhage, venodilation (nitroglycerin), positive pressure ventilation (reduces venous return).

2. Afterload — the load the ventricle must overcome during ejection. For the LV, this is primarily the systemic vascular resistance (SVR) and aortic pressure. Clinically, Laplace's law applies: wall stress = the true afterload. Increased by: hypertension, aortic stenosis, vasoconstrictors. Decreased by: vasodilators (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, hydralazine), aortic regurgitation (runoff lowers diastolic BP → lower afterload paradoxically).

3. Contractility (Inotropy) — the intrinsic ability of the myocardium to contract, independent of preload and afterload. Measured by: EF, ESPVR slope, dp/dt_max (rate of pressure rise during IVC). Increased by: sympathetic stimulation (β₁ receptors), catecholamines (dobutamine, adrenaline), digoxin (cardiac glycoside), calcium, exercise. Decreased by: heart failure, cardiomyopathy, ischemia, acidosis, hypoxia, negative inotropes (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers acutely, most anesthetic agents).

Fick's Principle — Cardiac Output Measurement
CO = VO₂ / (CaO₂ − CvO₂)
VO₂ = oxygen consumption (mL/min, ~250 mL/min at rest) | CaO₂ = arterial oxygen content (mL/dL) | CvO₂ = mixed venous oxygen content (mL/dL) | (CaO₂ − CvO₂) = arteriovenous O₂ difference

Normal: CO = 250 / (20 − 15) mL/dL × 10 = 250/50 = 5 L/min

In the ICU, CO is measured by: (1) thermodilution via Swan-Ganz catheter (gold standard); (2) transpulmonary thermodilution (PiCCO system); (3) arterial pulse contour analysis; (4) echocardiography (VTI method); (5) Fick's principle (using measured VO₂ and A-V O₂ difference).

ScvO₂ (central venous O₂ saturation, from CVC) normally >70%. If ScvO₂ <70% → tissues extracting more O₂ (↑demand or ↓supply → shock). ScvO₂ >80% in sepsis → pathological shunting/inability to extract O₂.

The RAAS — Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

Etymology Renin: from Latin ren (kidney) — the enzyme is produced by kidney cells. Angiotensin: from angio- (vessel) + tensin (tension) — it tenses (constricts) blood vessels. Aldosterone: aldo- (aldehyde group in its structure) + -sterone (steroid hormone) — the "most potent" mineralocorticoid. The RAAS was described progressively: renin in 1898 (Tigerstedt and Bergman), angiotensin in 1939 (Braun-Menéndez and Page independently), aldosterone in 1954 (Simpson, Tait, Grundy).

The RAAS is the master long-term regulator of blood pressure, fluid balance, and cardiac remodeling. It is activated by: decreased renal perfusion pressure, decreased sodium delivery to the macula densa, and sympathetic stimulation (β₁ receptors on juxtaglomerular cells). Its overactivation is a key driver of hypertension, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular remodeling.

The Local (Tissue) RAAS

Beyond the circulating system, all components of the RAAS are expressed locally in the heart, brain, blood vessels, adipose tissue, and kidneys. Local cardiac Ang II contributes to cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and arrhythmogenesis — independent of systemic blood pressure. This is why ACE inhibitors and ARBs benefit heart failure patients even when they are already normotensive — they block local tissue RAAS-driven remodeling. (Ferrario, NEJM 2004)

Heart Failure — Pathophysiology & Management

Etymology Heart failure (HF): not actually cardiac "failure" in the sense of cardiac arrest, but an inability of the heart to pump sufficient blood to meet the body's metabolic needs (or doing so only at elevated filling pressures). Also called congestive heart failure (CHF) — from Latin congestus = accumulated/piled up, referring to the congestion of fluid in lungs (left HF) or periphery (right HF). The older term "dropsy" (from Greek hydrops = watery) referred to edema before the cardiac mechanism was understood.

Heart failure affects over 64 million people worldwide and carries a 5-year mortality of ~50% — worse than most cancers. It is the leading cause of hospitalization in people over 65 years old. Understanding its pathophysiology is essential for every clinician.

HFpEF (preserved EF) ≥50% Diastolic dysfunction: impaired relaxation/filling Hypertension, diabetes, obesity, aging, amyloid

Neurohormonal Activation — The Vicious Cycle

When cardiac output falls, two compensatory systems activate:

  1. Sympathetic nervous system: ↑norepinephrine → ↑HR, ↑contractility, vasoconstriction (↑afterload), RAAS activation. Short-term: beneficial (maintains BP). Long-term: toxic — norepinephrine directly causes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, calcium overload, downregulation of β₁ receptors, arrhythmias.
  2. RAAS: ↑Ang II → vasoconstriction, ↑aldosterone → Na⁺/H₂O retention (↑preload), cardiac fibrosis. Short-term: maintains BP and preload. Long-term: progressive remodeling, fibrosis, worsening HF.

Both systems are initially adaptive but become maladaptive — the essence of HF. This is why blocking both (β-blockers + ACEi/ARBi + aldosterone antagonists + ARNi) is the cornerstone of HFrEF therapy.

Clinical Application — The Four Pillars of HFrEF
  • 1. ARNi or ACEi/ARB: Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) reduces CV death by 20% vs. enalapril (PARADIGM-HF). If not tolerated, ACEi or ARB. Target: lowest tolerated dose that achieves BP control without symptomatic hypotension.
  • 2. Beta-blockers: Carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or bisoprolol. Reduce mortality by ~35% in HFrEF. Mechanism: reverse β₁ downregulation, reduce sympatho-toxic effects, slow HR (↑diastole, ↑coronary filling), reduce arrhythmias. Start at LOW doses in STABLE patients; acute decompensated HF = withhold or reduce.
  • 3. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA): Spironolactone or eplerenone. Reduce mortality ~25% (RALES/EMPHASIS-HF). Block aldosterone-driven fibrosis and Na⁺ retention. Monitor K⁺ closely (risk of hyperkalemia, especially with ACEi/ARB).
  • 4. SGLT2 inhibitors (Gliflozins): Dapagliflozin (DAPA-HF) and empagliflozin (EMPEROR-Reduced) reduce CV death/HF hospitalization by ~25% — in ALL EF ranges. Mechanism: osmotic diuresis, reduced preload, reduced intracellular Na⁺ (NHE1 inhibition), mitochondrial protection. Now recommended regardless of diabetes status.

Additionally: diuretics (loop diuretics: furosemide) for symptom control (no mortality benefit but essential for decongestion); ivabradine if HR ≥70 on maximal beta-blocker; digoxin for rate control in HF + AF; hydralazine + isosorbide dinitrate in ACEi/ARB-intolerant patients (especially Black patients — A-HeFT trial).

Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)

Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS) encompass a spectrum of clinical conditions caused by acute reduction of coronary blood flow, usually due to plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombus formation. ACS is the leading cause of death in high-income countries.

Atherosclerosis — The Molecular Process

Atherosclerosis (from Greek athere = porridge + skleros = hard) is the fundamental process underlying ACS. Key steps:

  1. Endothelial dysfunction: Risk factors (hypertension, smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes) cause oxidative stress → ↓NO production → ↑ICAM-1, VCAM-1 expression → monocyte adhesion
  2. Lipid accumulation: oxidized LDL (oxLDL) enters the intima → engulfed by macrophages (via SR-A, CD36 scavenger receptors) → foam cells → fatty streak
  3. Plaque formation: Smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation → fibrous cap formation over lipid core. Stable plaque = thick fibrous cap; Vulnerable plaque = thin fibrous cap, large lipid core, many macrophages (MMPs degrade the cap → prone to rupture)
  4. Plaque rupture/erosion: Physical stress or MMP-mediated cap erosion → subendothelial collagen and tissue factor exposed → platelet adhesion (via GP Ib-von Willebrand Factor) and activation → platelet aggregation (ADP, TXA₂) → fibrin clot formation → occlusion

STEMI Management — Time is Myocardium

STEMI: The Golden Hour

Primary PCI (Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) is the preferred reperfusion strategy. Target: door-to-balloon time <90 minutes (or <120 min if transfer required). For every 10 minutes of delay, approximately 1.6 additional lives lost per 1,000 patients treated.

If PCI unavailable within 120 minutes: Fibrinolysis (tenecteplase, alteplase) — a thrombolytic agent that converts plasminogen → plasmin → fibrin degradation. Must be given within 12 hours of symptom onset. Absolute contraindications: prior intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke <3 months, active bleeding, aortic dissection.

Antiplatelet: Aspirin (300 mg loading dose) + P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor 180 mg or prasugrel 60 mg for PCI; clopidogrel if fibrinolysis). Ticagrelor and prasugrel are more potent and faster than clopidogrel. Anticoagulation: UFH (unfractionated heparin), enoxaparin, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux.

Shock — Cardiovascular Failure States

Etymology Shock: possibly from Old French choquer (to strike violently) or Dutch schokken (to push/jolt). In medicine, shock means inadequate tissue perfusion resulting in cellular hypoxia and organ dysfunction — not the colloquial meaning of emotional shock. Vasopressor: from Latin vas (vessel) + pressus (pressure) — drugs that increase blood pressure by constricting blood vessels.

Shock is defined as tissue hypoperfusion causing cellular oxygen deficit. It is not simply low blood pressure — though hypotension (MAP <65 mmHg in adults) is the most common clinical marker. Diagnosis requires evidence of hypoperfusion: elevated lactate (>2 mmol/L), altered mental status, oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h), cold/mottled skin, or elevated ScvO₂.

Septic Shock — Molecular Pathophysiology

Septic shock is the most common form of shock in the ICU. Bacteria release lipopolysaccharide (LPS, endotoxin) or other pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) → recognized by toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on macrophages and endothelial cells → NFκB activation → massive cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) → inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) → massive NO production → profound vasodilation → low SVR → distributive shock.

Additionally: endothelial glycocalyx destruction → capillary leak → third-spacing → hypovolemia despite high venous pressures. Myocardial depression (direct cytokine toxicity) occurs in ~40% of septic shock. Mitochondrial dysfunction → impaired O₂ utilization (cytopathic hypoxia) → elevated lactate even with normal/high DO₂.

Clinical Application — Surviving Sepsis Campaign Bundles
Hour-1 Bundle (Sepsis-3 / Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2018)
  • 1. Measure lactate (repeat if initial >2 mmol/L)
  • 2. Blood cultures × 2 BEFORE antibiotics
  • 3. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics within 1 hour of recognition
  • 4. Crystalloid 30 mL/kg IV for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L
  • 5. Vasopressors if MAP <65 mmHg despite fluid: Norepinephrine first-line (α₁ + β₁ → vasoconstriction + mild inotropy). Target MAP ≥65 mmHg.

Vasopressin (0.03–0.04 units/min) is added if norepinephrine dose is high (>0.25 mcg/kg/min). Dobutamine for cardiogenic component (low CO despite adequate MAP). Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day continuous for refractory shock (norepinephrine ≥0.25 mcg/kg/min). Stress-dose steroids reduce time on vasopressors but do not improve mortality in all-comers.

Mean Arterial Pressure
MAP = DBP + (SBP − DBP) / 3 = DBP + PP/3
MAP = Mean Arterial Pressure | SBP = Systolic BP | DBP = Diastolic BP | PP = Pulse Pressure

Alternatively: MAP ≈ (SBP + 2×DBP) / 3 (because diastole lasts ~2/3 of the cardiac cycle).
Normal: MAP 70–100 mmHg. Target in septic shock: ≥65 mmHg (or ≥70–85 in patients with chronic hypertension or known cerebrovascular disease — SEPSISPAM trial).

MAP is the perfusion pressure that matters for organ blood flow — organs are perfused proportionally to MAP − venous/compartmental pressure. Brain perfusion: CPP = MAP − ICP (intracranial pressure).

Echocardiography — The Cardiologist's Stethoscope

Etymology Echocardiography: from Greek ēchō (echo) + kardia (heart) + graphein (to write). Uses ultrasound waves (1.5–10 MHz) reflecting off cardiac structures — the time delay of reflected waves (echoes) creates an image. Key Echo Measurements

  • EF (Ejection Fraction): EF = (EDV − ESV) / EDV × 100%. Normal ≥55%. Measured by Simpson's biplane method or 3D echo.
  • LV dimensions: Normal LVID_d (diastolic internal diameter): 3.9–5.3 cm (women), 4.2–5.9 cm (men). LVED volume normal: 56–104 mL (women), 67–155 mL (men).
  • Aortic valve area (AVA): Measured by continuity equation. AVA <1.0 cm² = severe aortic stenosis. Mean gradient >40 mmHg = severe AS.
  • E/e' ratio: Ratio of mitral inflow E velocity (from PW Doppler) to myocardial e' velocity (from tissue Doppler at the septal annulus). E/e' <8 = normal LV filling pressure. E/e' 8–14 = indeterminate. E/e' >14 = elevated LV filling pressure (diastolic dysfunction) — key for diagnosing HFpEF.
  • IVC size/collapsibility: IVC diameter <21 mm + collapsibility >50% with sniff → low RA pressure (~3 mmHg). IVC >21 mm + <50% collapsibility → elevated RA pressure (≥10 mmHg). Used to guide fluid management in ICU/shock.

Invasive Hemodynamic Monitoring

Clinical Application — The Pulmonary Artery Catheter (Swan-Ganz)

The PAC (Pulmonary Artery Catheter) is a balloon-tipped flow-directed catheter floated through the right heart into the pulmonary artery. Despite controversy about mortality benefit (ESCAPE trial showed no benefit in HF), it provides invaluable hemodynamic data in complex ICU cases:

  • CVP (Central Venous Pressure) — RA pressure → preload
  • PA systolic/diastolic/mean pressures → pulmonary hypertension
  • PCWP (Pulmonary Capillary Wedge Pressure) → LA pressure, LV preload
  • CO by thermodilution → cardiac output, cardiac index
  • SvO₂ (mixed venous saturation) from PA → global oxygen delivery/consumption balance
  • SVR calculation: SVR = (MAP − CVP) / CO × 80 (dynes·s·cm⁻⁵). Normal: 700–1,200 d·s·cm⁻⁵
  • PVR (Pulmonary Vascular Resistance): PVR = (mPAP − PCWP) / CO × 80. Normal: <250 d·s·cm⁻⁵
